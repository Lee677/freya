freyacad

Parametric solid modelling that runs entirely in your browser.

Manual — last revised 24 August 2026

Start here

freyacad is a small CAD program with a real geometry kernel. You sketch on a plane, turn sketches into solids, and export STL for printing or STEP for other CAD. There is nothing to install and no account.

The workflow is the same one every mainstream CAD package uses, and it is worth knowing before you touch anything:

  1. Sketch a closed profile on a flat plane.
  2. Dimension it until it can't move.
  3. Extrude or revolve that profile into a solid.
  4. Repeat — each new feature builds on the last, and the whole stack rebuilds whenever you change something earlier in it.

That last point is what "parametric" means. Your part is not a mesh you sculpted; it is a recipe. Change a dimension near the bottom of the tree and everything above it is recalculated from scratch.

Good to know

Nothing leaves your machine. There is no server, no upload, and no telemetry. The geometry kernel runs as WebAssembly inside the page, so your part exists only in the tab until you save or export it — which also means closing the tab loses your work. Save early.

What you need

  • A desktop or laptop browser — Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari, reasonably current.
  • WebGL2 and WebAssembly, both of which those browsers have had for years.
  • A mouse with a wheel. A trackpad works, but a wheel makes zooming far nicer.

Phones and tablets are blocked deliberately. The kernel is a ~13 MB download and the interface needs a hover cursor and real screen space, so rather than let it half-work on a phone, freyacad shows a friendly note and stops.

The fastest way to learn it

Press Demo in the toolbar and pick one. Each one builds a finished model in front of you, feature by feature, so you can watch the tree fill in and see what a normal modelling session looks like. Then open the tree items and see how each was made. Whichever you pick is the one that comes back the next time you launch; Hide the demo at the foot of the menu starts you empty instead.

Views & projection

Normal to — Ctrl+8

Looks square down whatever plane is relevant: the sketch you have open, or a plane you have selected in the tree, or the Top plane if you haven't picked anything. This is the view you want for drawing and for reading dimensions — it's the only one where what you see on screen matches the true shape of the profile.

Isometric — Ctrl+7

The standard three-quarter view. Inside a sketch it tilts the camera off the sketch plane without closing the sketch, so you can see what you're drawing in context and then drop back with Ctrl+8.

Perspective and orthographic — Ctrl+5

The Persp / Ortho button swaps how the scene is projected.

  • Perspective converges, like an eye or a camera. Distant parts get smaller. It reads as more natural and gives a better sense of depth.
  • Orthographic keeps parallel edges parallel and holds scale across depth. Equal lengths measure equal wherever they sit, which is why engineering drawings use it, and why it's the projection to check a part in when you want to trust your eyes.

Both cameras share one orbit, so the swap holds your position and framing — only the projection changes.

Making a sketch

Add → Sketch, then pick a plane: Top, Front, Right, or a flat face of an existing solid. The camera flies round to look straight at it and the drawing tools appear.

While a sketch is open you'll see:

  • The origin — a small pair of arrows at 0,0. This is the anchor of your part; geometry tied back to it can't drift. The arrows flash when the sketch opens and whenever you resize, then fade so they don't clutter the view.
  • Plane traces — dashed, colour-keyed lines showing where the other two datum planes slice through the one you're on. They're the sketch equivalent of a centre line: extremely useful for keeping things symmetrical, and you can snap to them, drop points on them and dimension to them.
  • The status chip — a live count of what's still loose.

To round or bevel every corner of the profile at once, right-click and pick Sketch corners… (with the other sketch entity tools) — a fillet/chamfer control appears for just that job, and stays out of the way otherwise.

The right-click menu, and rearranging it

Right-clicking in a sketch gives four groups: Sketch (Finish, Finish + extrude, Cancel) first, since finishing is the commonest reason to open it; then Sketch entities, Relations and View.

Customise tools… at the foot of that menu opens a dialog where you can drag any tool into another group, reorder it, or drop it into Hidden to take it off both the menu and the dock. It has a tab per menu — Sketch, Model and Assembly — so every right-click menu in the program is arranged the same way, and each chip carries the tool's own icon so you can find it by sight. Your layout is remembered by the browser; Reset to default puts it back.

Finish with Enter or the Finish button. Esc backs out of whatever you're mid-way through, and again to leave the sketch.

Closed profiles

Extrude and revolve need a closed loop. A chain of lines that doesn't return to its start has no inside, so there's nothing to give thickness to. If a feature refuses to build, an open profile is the first thing to check.

Worth knowing

The two axis-aligned rectangles are stored as rectangles, so they keep the width and height dimensions and the two corner handles. The two three-point ones can sit at any angle, which that storage can't express, so they commit as a closed four-sided profile — every corner is draggable, and you dimension the sides like any other lines.

Snapping

As the cursor nears something worth catching, it locks on and the target lights up before you click, so you always know what you're about to get. It snaps to:

  • the sketch origin;
  • plane traces, and the point where two of them cross;
  • endpoints of lines you've already drawn — these glow as you approach.

Dragging catches the same things. Pull an existing point around with the pointer and it hunts for the origin, the trace crossings, the traces and every other vertex, exactly as the drawing tools do — the marker turns teal when it has hold of something, and the status line names what it caught when you let go.

Snapping isn't cosmetic. A point snapped to the origin is genuinely attached to it, and that costs it its freedom to move — which is how a sketch becomes defined without you dimensioning every last thing. Dragging a loose endpoint onto the origin will define it the same way, mid-edit.

Snapping reaches a fixed distance on screen, not in millimetres, so zooming in tightens it: work up close and the cursor only catches what is genuinely near.

The cursor also shows a small glyph of the active tool up and to the right, so you can tell at a glance whether you're about to draw a line or a circle.

Dimensions

Press D or pick the dimension tool, then click what you want to measure. A greyed preview with real leaders and arrowheads follows the cursor, so you can see the dimension forming before you commit. Type a number and press Enter and the geometry moves to match — dimensions here drive the model, they don't just report it.

To dimensionClick
The length of a lineThe line, then Enter a value
Between two pointsOne endpoint, then another
A point back to the originThe point, then the origin
The angle between two linesOne line, then the other
The angle to a datum planeA line, then a plane trace (either order)
A point's distance to a datum planeThe point, then a plane trace (either order)
A circleThe circle — you get its diameter

The plane traces count as ground: they can't move, so a dimension to one costs only the sketched entity its freedom. That is what makes a line drawn from the origin fully defined by two dimensions — its length, and its angle to a plane. Two distances to the two traces do the same job for a single point, which is the reliable way to pin one down: a single diagonal distance leaves it free to swing on a circle.

Drag any dimension to reposition its leader line; long chains get unreadable fast if they all sit on top of each other. Values accept up to four decimal places, and the input box grows to fit what you type.

A dimension only changes when you type

This is a rule the whole program keeps, not a habit. Nothing — dragging a point, making a line horizontal, adding a relation — may alter a value you typed. If an operation cannot be carried out with every dimension still reading what it says, the operation is undone and you are told, rather than the numbers being quietly rewritten: "Horizontal would change a dimension — the sketch would be over-defined. Edit or delete the dimension first."

Driven dimensions

If a new dimension would over-define the sketch — what it measures is already fully decided — freyacad warns you and offers to add it as driven instead: a grey reference reading shown in parentheses, like (12.5 mm). A driven dimension follows the geometry and never moves it, costs no freedom in the count, and can be erased like any other. It's the honest way to keep a measurement visible on a sketch that's already fully in charge of itself.

Current limitation

Dimensions are applied one at a time, not solved together. Each one moves geometry to satisfy itself, which can disturb a dimension you set earlier — add an angle and a length you already fixed may shift. Work outward from the origin, dimension in the order you'd actually build the shape, and check earlier values afterwards. A proper constraint solver is the fix, and is the biggest single improvement still to come.

Constraints & degrees of freedom

The chip in the top-left corner is doing real arithmetic, not guessing. Every entity carries a number of free values — a line has four (two endpoints, two coordinates each), a circle three, a rectangle four — and every dimension, snap or constraint removes one. What is left over is the degrees of freedom: the number of independent ways your sketch can still deform.

When it hits zero the sketch is fully defined. That is the state you want before building on it, because nothing can shift underneath a feature later.

Only the origin and the datum planes count as ground. A shape can have every side dimensioned and every corner constrained to its neighbours and still slide around the plane as a rigid unit — internal relationships alone never fully define anything. Until something chains the shape to the origin or a plane (a coincident point, a distance to a plane, an angle to a plane), the count floors at the freedoms the whole shape retains, and the chip stays honest about it.

The constraints

Five constraints live in the tool dock. Horizontal and vertical work either way round:

  • Select first — click a line with the pointer, then click the H or V icon.
  • Tool first — click the H or V icon, then click the line.

Horizontal and vertical are permanent: the line straightens and then stays that way — drag it and it slides or stretches, but it will not turn. A small green H or V badge appears up-right of the line's middle; the only way the line's angle changes again is deleting that badge (click it and press Delete, or click it with the eraser).

Coincident (⊕) locks two points together — click one point, then the point to lock it to, and from then on they move as one. Lock a point to the origin the same way to pin it to ground. Its badge sits by the joined point and deletes like the others.

Clicking a circle or polygon anywhere on its outline means its centre, so the same tool makes two of them concentric — no hunting for the little centre dot. That pairing badges itself ◎, since two shapes sharing a centre is worth being able to see and undo.

Snaps become constraints by themselves. Draw or drop a point onto the origin and a ⊕ appears; onto a plane trace and an ∈ (on-plane) appears — the point can then slide along the plane but never off it. Release a dragged point onto another point and the join is stored as a coincident. What snapped, holds — until you delete the badge.

Badges appear only for relationships you could not otherwise read off the sketch: ground (origin, plane) and concentric. Two line ends joined to each other show nothing — the join is plain to see, and a marker on every corner would only be clutter.

Parallel (∥) and equal (=) relate two lines, so they always want a reference first: click the icon (or have a line selected when you click it), click the line to copy from, then click the line to change. Parallel matches its direction; equal matches its length. Both persist exactly like H and V — the badge sits on the following line, and deleting it is what dissolves the relationship.

Every constraint holds through everything: drags, other constraints, and typed dimension changes all settle together, so a line that says H is horizontal — if a dimension edit would bend it, the free geometry moves instead.

Constraints respect what's already anchored. A line drawn from the origin is attached there, so making it vertical swings its free end round — it won't drag the anchored end off its datum. Only when neither end is held does the line pivot about its middle.

These are cheap and worth using freely. A line pinned horizontal loses a degree of freedom that no dimension then has to spend.

Dragging respects your dimensions

Only the origin and the datum planes are truly fixed. Everything else is exactly as free as its constraints leave it, and dragging honours that:

  • Undimensioned geometry drags anywhere.
  • Dimensioned geometry still drags — but the numbers hold. While you pull a point, every dimension keeps re-imposing its value. A line with a length dimension swings about its far end at exactly that length; two lines with an angle between them move and rotate as a pair, the angle intact. A drag can never silently rewrite a value you typed.
  • Fully defined geometry won't drag at all. Once every freedom is spent against the origin and planes, there is nowhere left to go — the point flashes red and the only way to move it is to edit a dimension.
  • And if a drag can't keep every number, it doesn't happen. When you let go, the sketch checks that every dimension still reads what you typed; a move that would have changed one is taken back, with a hint saying so. Typing is the only door a dimension's value can change through.

Points from different lines sitting on the same spot count as joined: drag a shared corner and everything meeting there moves together.

Why "fully defined" matters

An underdefined sketch isn't broken — you can extrude it perfectly well. The risk is later: change something upstream and an unconstrained profile can wander to a shape you never intended, and the first you'll know is a rebuild that looks wrong. Fully defined means the part can only change the way you asked it to.

Splines

The spline tool draws a smooth curve through the points you click — click each point in turn, and each one lands as you click it. Click the first point again to close the curve; Esc or Enter ends an open one, and Backspace steps back a point while you're drawing. A closed spline is a profile like any other: extrude it, cut with it, revolve it.

The curve is a centripetal fit, which is the one that doesn't overshoot or loop back on itself when your points are unevenly spaced. It passes exactly through every point, so what you clicked is where the curve goes.

Editing one

Spline points are ordinary sketch points. They drag, they snap, they take dimensions and constraints, they count against the degrees of freedom, and a dimension on one holds through a drag exactly like anywhere else. Select the spline with the pointer and it shows its workings: the control polygon it was fitted through, dashed, and a handle at every point.

  • Move a point — drag it.
  • Change the curve at a point — drag either end of that point's handle. The bar sets both the direction the curve leaves in and how hard it pulls; both ends stay in line, so the curve is always smooth through the point. A handle you have set yourself goes amber; the grey ones are the fit's own.
  • Insert a point — right-click on the curve where you want it.
  • Delete a point — right-click near it. A spline keeps a floor of two points (three if closed); below that, delete the whole spline.
  • Reset a handle, or all of them, from the same menu — the fit takes over again.
  • Open or close it, and hide the control polygons, from that menu too.
  • Delete with the spline selected removes the whole curve — unlike a chain of lines, a spline is one thing.

Splines mirror, take part in dynamic mirror, and can be construction geometry like anything else.

Where this stops

Not everything SolidWorks does with splines is here. These are through-point splines — SolidWorks' plain Spline tool. There are no control-point (style) splines, no equation-driven curves, no fitting a spline onto existing geometry, no curvature combs, and no tangent or curvature relations between a spline end and a neighbouring line: the handle is set by hand, not constrained to anything.

Editing a sketch

All of this needs the pointer tool active.

  • Move a point — drag any endpoint, as long as it's still blue. The view stays put while you drag, and the point snaps to the origin, the plane traces and other vertices on its way past.
  • Resize a circle or a polygon — drag its outline, anywhere along it. There's no special handle to find: the whole rim is the handle, and the shape follows the cursor from wherever you took hold. A single click on the outline selects the shape instead.
  • Spin a polygon — drag one of its corners. Corners resize and rotate; the flats between them only resize.
  • Delete — click a line to select it, then Delete. Or use the eraser to remove individual segments and dimensions.
  • Select several at once — drag a box over empty space. Everything completely inside it is caught, lines and dimensions alike; then press Delete or pick the eraser to remove the lot. (Hold Shift and drag if you want to orbit the view instead.)
  • Change a dimension — click its value and type a new one.
  • Turn a line into construction geometry — select it and tick Construction geometry. Ticking one segment of a longer chain splits that segment out first, so only the line you picked changes.

To edit a sketch you've already finished, find it in the tree and open it from the right-click menu. Everything downstream rebuilds when you're done.

A profile can be made of several pieces

A closed profile doesn't have to be one closed run. A line and a spline that meet at both ends enclose an area just as well, and so do four separate lines drawn as four separate runs — all of them extrude, cut and revolve. What matters is that the ends actually meet: snap each new run onto the end of the last, or join them with a coincident relation.

Where three or more ends meet at one point the shape is ambiguous, so nothing is guessed and no profile is made there. A run that dead-ends is simply left out — a stray line next to a closed rectangle doesn't stop the rectangle being a profile.

Features

Everything lives under Add. Features that need something they haven't got — a sketch, or an existing solid — stay greyed out until it exists.

Select a feature in the tree and its parameters appear in a panel — depth, radius, count, spacing. Edit them and the model rebuilds.

Sketches with more than one shape

A sketch can hold several separate closed regions — say a hexagon with a hole and a triangle beside it. Extrude, cut and revolve normally use them all. When there is more than one, the feature's panel lists them as contours with a tick box each, so you can build from just the ones you want. Leave them all ticked for the usual behaviour.

A shape only counts as a contour if its outline is closed. An open chain has no inside, so it makes no face — that is the usual reason a shape seems to be ignored.

Worth knowing

Parameter-panel edits are covered by undo. Pressing Done records one step, so Ctrl+Z puts the old depth or radius back. Pressing Cancel, or Done without having changed anything, records nothing — so undo never fills up with entries for panels you only looked at.

Worth knowing

A distance × angle chamfer is built as its equivalent distance pair — the run along the second face is the first distance times the tangent of the angle. That is exact wherever the two faces meet square, which is every edge you would put an angle on. Open CASCADE has its own distance-angle routine, but it fails on the circular edges this app produces, and the distance pair does not.

Fillets and chamfers are the usual place a rebuild fails, and it's rarely a bug: a radius bigger than the material it has to blend into has no valid solution. Reduce it, or put the fillet earlier in the tree before the geometry gets tight.

Naming things pays off quickly. Extrude 3 tells you nothing six features later; Mounting boss does. The demo part is named this way for exactly that reason.

Sketches nest inside the feature built from them, since once consumed they're an ingredient rather than a step of their own. They're hidden by default; right-click → Show displays one in yellow when you need to see what a feature was made from. An eye icon on the row marks anything currently visible.

Datum geometry

The three planes — Top, Front, Right — meet at the origin and are the reference everything else hangs off. They sit at the top of the tree, above your features, because they exist before the part does.

The three origin axes — X, Y and Z — sit just below them in the tree, and work the same way: click to show or hide, colour-keyed red, green and blue. Like the planes they are always there and never part of the recipe.

They're hidden until wanted; right-click one and toggle its visibility. You can also add your own:

  • Add plane — a new sketching plane set at an angle to one of the three. Pick the plane to start from, which of its two in-plane axes to tilt about, and the angle. The result still passes through that axis, so it's an angled plane rather than a floating one. This is how you sketch somewhere that isn't a face of the model.
  • Add axis — a reference line, useful as the spin axis for a revolve or a circular pattern. Its panel asks how the axis is defined: two planes (the line where they meet), normal to a plane through a point, or a plain position and direction. Parallel planes are refused — they never meet.

Undo

Ctrl+Z steps back, Ctrl+Y forward, up to 60 steps. Undo captures the whole document, so stepping back into a sketch reopens that sketch as it was.

Parameter-panel edits count too: one step per Done that actually changed something. A new feature still takes a single press to remove, panel edit and all.

Saving & opening

Save writes a .sketchcad file — plain JSON holding the entire recipe: every sketch, dimension, feature and parameter. It's small, diffable, and opens back up fully editable. This is your working format; use it for anything you might revisit.

Open loads one back. New part starts fresh.

Importing STEP and STL

The same Open button also takes .step / .stp and .stl files. They come into the current part as an import feature in the tree — the file's bytes travel inside your .sketchcad, so the part still saves, reopens and replays as one self-contained recipe.

  • STEP arrives as true B-rep solid geometry, read by the same kernel that writes it. An imported STEP body is a first-class citizen: fillet its edges, cut it, mirror it, pattern it, export it again.
  • STL is only ever triangles, so it arrives as a faceted body. It's exactly right for viewing, placing and re-exporting; fillets and smooth-surface work need real surfaces that a mesh simply doesn't have.

Files over 30 MB are refused — they'd make the saved part unwieldy. Decimate large scans first.

Save early

There is no autosave and no recovery. Everything lives in the tab, so a crash, a closed window or a stray refresh takes the part with it. Save whenever you've made progress you'd be annoyed to redo.

Units

The Units button switches what dimensions are displayed and typed in. It changes presentation, not the model — the underlying geometry is unaffected.

Exporting

STL — for 3D printing

A triangle mesh. Every slicer reads it, and it's what you want for printing. Curves become facets, so a cylinder is really a many-sided prism; fine for printing, lossy as a master copy. STL carries no feature history — keep the .sketchcad file too.

STEP — for other CAD

Real B-rep geometry written straight out of the kernel: planes stay planes, cylinders stay cylinders, fillets stay analytic blend surfaces. Nothing is tessellated. This is the format to hand to a machine shop, to import into Fusion or SolidWorks, or to keep as a neutral archive. If you're choosing between the two and not printing, choose STEP.

Assemblies

New assembly starts a document that holds several parts rather than one. Insert brings a saved part in as a component; select one and Delete removes it. Assemblies save as .asmcad.

Drag a component to slide it on the ground plane; hold Shift to raise or lower it instead. Right-click for the component menu — position, fix in place, remove, and the two mates. A component that is fixed won't move, and nothing mated to it can pull it: the first part you insert is fixed automatically, so everything else lands relative to it. Unfix a part and any mate that had nowhere to give settles straight away.

The origin

An assembly has the same origin a part does: Top, Front and Right planes and the X, Y and Z axes, at the head of the tree, click to show or hide. They are fixed — nothing moves them — which makes them the natural thing to build an assembly around. Parts can be mated to them, so you can sit a part on the Top plane or run it down the Y axis without needing another part to reference.

Mates

Both mates work by picking faces, one on each part. Choose the mate, click the face on the part you want to move, then click the face it should meet. Faces light up as the cursor passes over them, the first pick stays highlighted in amber, and Esc cancels. If a face is the wrong sort for the mate you chose, the pick is refused and says so rather than doing something surprising.

The second pick can be the origin instead of another part: an origin plane for a coincident mate, an origin axis for a concentric one. Whichever sort the mate needs appears on its own as soon as you start it, so there's nothing to turn on first. The first pick is always a face on the part you want to move — the origin can't travel, so it can't be the thing that moves.

MatePickDoes
ConcentricTwo round faces, or a round face and an origin axisPuts the two on a common axis — a pin into a hole, a boss into a counterbore, a shaft onto Y. The part stays free to slide along that axis and spin about it.
CoincidentTwo flat faces, or a flat face and an origin planeSets the faces against each other, face to face. The part stays free to slide about on that plane.

Faces are recovered from the part's own geometry, so a hole reads as a hole and its diameter is reported back when the mate lands. If both parts are fixed, nothing can move and the mate says so — and since the origin never moves, mating a fixed part to it is refused the same way. Otherwise the part whose face you picked first is the one that travels.

Mates hold

A mate is a relation, not a one-off move. Once it's on, it stays true: drag the part afterwards and it slides within whatever freedoms the mate leaves it, rather than coming loose. A pin mated concentric into a hole runs up and down that hole and spins in it, but will not come out sideways. Add a coincident mate on its end face as well and it's located outright, with only the spin left. Mates to the origin hold in exactly the same way — a part run down the Y axis stays on it however you drag.

Dragging a part tows its neighbours. The part under the cursor keeps its ground and anything mated to it that isn't fixed follows along, so a stack moves as a stack. Typing a position into the panel works the same way, and the boxes are corrected to where the part actually ended up — what the mates allow beats what you typed.

Every mate is listed under Mates in the tree, with an × to delete it; deleting one leaves the parts where they are. A mate that can't currently be met — usually because both its parts have been fixed — is marked with a ! and named in the status bar, along with how far out it is. If a new mate can't be met alongside the ones already there, it is refused and nothing moves, rather than half-applied.

Mates are saved with the assembly. Reopening the file replays the relations and solves the positions from them, so a mated assembly comes back mated.

Drawings

New drawing makes an engineering drawing of what you have modelled. It replaces the 3D view with a flat sheet of paper — nothing rotates, nothing orbits, the model is put away until you come back to it. The same button takes you back and forth; the drawing keeps its layout, and every view is re-projected from the model as it now stands each time you return. Change a dimension on the part, come back, and the drawing has changed with it.

Views

Add view offers front, top, right, left, bottom, back and isometric. Each is a real orthographic projection of the model's edges, not a picture of the screen: edges you can see draw solid, edges the solid stands in front of draw dashed as hidden detail, and Hidden turns that detail off. Drag a view to place it; the tree lists them with an × to remove one.

An isometric view is shaded by default, with its edges drawn on top — a wire cage of an isometric shows every edge of the far side straight through the near one, which is the one view where that helps nobody. The orthographic views stay line drawings, as a drawing should. Shaded / wireframe in the sheet menu switches the selected view either way.

Pull a view straight off another one. Hover a view and a dashed frame appears round it; drag from that frame and you get the view that belongs on the side you pulled towards — right for the right view, up for the top, a corner for isometric. It lands in line with its parent and stays there: drag a projected view and it slides along its one free axis, drag the parent and its projections come along. Which view you get follows the sheet's convention: third angle puts the top view above and the right view to the right, first angle the other way about, and the title block carries the matching symbol. Dragging from inside a view still just moves it, and Add view is still there when you want to name one.

Model… draws a saved part instead of the one you have open — pick a .sketchcad file and every view re-projects from it, without disturbing your work.

Dimensioning

Dimension (or D) arms the dimension tool. Click a corner on a view, click a second corner, then click where the line should sit. Corners snap, so a dimension always means the corner and not "roughly there". Where you place the line chooses the kind, as a smart dimension does: put it above or below and you asked for the horizontal distance, out to the side and you asked for the vertical one.

The corner the next click would take glows as the cursor passes it, so you are never aiming at a snap you cannot see. The number is measured off the model, not off the paper: change the sheet scale to 1:2 and the drawing halves while the dimension still reads 40. Dimensions belong to their view, so they travel with it when you drag it and go with it when you delete it. Drag a dimension to move it where you want it; click one and press Delete to remove it. Esc puts the tool down, and again leaves the drawing.

The drawing tools

Right-click anywhere on the sheet for every drawing command, grouped the same way the sketch menu is, with the same floating dock along the top of the sheet. Customise tools… at the foot of that menu opens the same dialog the other modes use — the drawing has its own tab, and dragging a command into another group, or into Hidden, rearranges the menu and the dock together.

Sheet, scale and output

Sheet size, orientation and scale are in that menu and dock too — the top toolbar stays out of the way in this mode, since everything the sheet needs is on the sheet.

Sheet sizes run A4 to A0, portrait or landscape. The scale is one you pick from the usual series, or fit, which chooses the largest standard scale that still gets everything on the page — a drawing is read at a stated scale, so it picks 1:2 rather than inventing 1:1.83. Whatever it lands on is printed in the title block.

Print opens the sheet at its true paper size — print it, or use your browser's "Save as PDF". Export SVG saves it as vectors at real millimetre dimensions, which opens in Illustrator, Inkscape, or a laser cutter's software.

Where this stops

Dimensions are linear — between two corners. There are no diameter or radius dimensions, no angular ones, and no notes, centre marks, centrelines, balloons or bill of materials. There are no section, detail or auxiliary views. The drawing is not saved with the part: the sheet lasts as long as the tab, so print or export it before you close.

Known limits

Stated plainly, so nothing surprises you mid-part:

  • No constraint solver in sketches yet. Dimensions apply one at a time and can disturb each other. See the note under Dimensions. (Assembly mates are solved together — that solver doesn't work on sketch geometry.)
  • No perpendicular constraint. Horizontal, vertical, parallel, equal, concentric and coincident are in; perpendicular isn't yet.
  • STL imports are faceted. A mesh has no true surfaces, so an imported STL can be viewed, placed and exported but not filleted or chamfered.
  • Desktop only, by design.
  • No autosave. The tab is the only copy until you save.
  • Drawings are linear dimensions only. No diameter, radius or angular dimensions, no notes or centre marks, no section or detail views, and the sheet isn't saved with the part. See Drawings.
  • Only two assembly mates. Concentric and coincident hold as real relations and are solved together, but distance, angle, parallel and the rest aren't there yet, and neither are joints or sub-assemblies.

About

freyacad is built on Open CASCADE — the same B-rep kernel behind FreeCAD — compiled to WebAssembly and running in the page. That's why STEP export is genuine solid geometry rather than a mesh dressed up as one. The interface is drawn with three.js. The whole application is a single HTML file with no build step and no framework.
