freyacad

Parametric solid modelling that runs entirely in your browser.

Manual — last revised 25 August 2026

Start here

freyacad is a small CAD program with a real geometry kernel. You sketch on a plane, turn sketches into solids, and print them — 3MF or STL for your slicer, true STEP for other CAD. There is nothing to install and no account.

The workflow is the same one every mainstream CAD package uses, and it is worth knowing before you touch anything:

  1. Sketch a closed profile on a flat plane.
  2. Dimension it until it can't move.
  3. Extrude or revolve that profile into a solid.
  4. Repeat — each new feature builds on the last, and the whole stack rebuilds whenever you change something earlier in it.

That last point is what "parametric" means. Your part is not a mesh you sculpted; it is a recipe. Change a dimension near the bottom of the tree and everything above it is recalculated from scratch.

Good to know

Nothing leaves your machine. There is no server, no upload, and no telemetry. The geometry kernel runs as WebAssembly inside the page, so your part exists only in the tab until you save or export it — which also means closing the tab loses your work. Save early.

What you need

  • A desktop or laptop browser — Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari, reasonably current.
  • WebGL2 and WebAssembly, both of which those browsers have had for years.
  • A mouse with a wheel. A trackpad works, but a wheel makes zooming far nicer.

Phones and tablets — iPads included — are blocked deliberately. The kernel is a ~13 MB download and the interface needs a hover cursor, a keyboard and real screen space, so rather than let it half-work on touch glass, freyacad shows a friendly note and stops.

The fastest way to learn it

Press Demo in the toolbar and pick one. Each one builds a finished model in front of you, feature by feature, so you can watch the tree fill in and see what a normal modelling session looks like. Then open the tree items and see how each was made. Whichever you pick is the one that comes back the next time you launch. Hide the demo at the foot of the menu starts you empty instead.

If a model feels slow

Almost all the time goes on boolean operations — the unions and cuts that join one feature to the last — and a boolean against a curved surface costs roughly the same whether that surface is simple or elaborate. So what makes a model slow is how many booleans it needs, not how complicated its shapes are. Two things follow from that, and both are worth knowing when you are laying a part out:

  • Consecutive features that all add material cost about the same as one. A run of merged extrudes is applied in a single boolean, and so is a run of cuts. Alternating add, cut, add, cut splits that run up and pays for each piece separately, so grouping your cuts together is genuinely faster than interleaving them.
  • Several profiles in one sketch beat several features. Twelve blades drawn in one sketch and extruded once are one operation; twelve separate extrudes are twelve.

Reopening a part you have already built is instant — the geometry is kept and reused, and selecting a feature or opening a sketch does not rebuild anything. It is only a genuine change to the recipe that costs kernel time.

Where you edit matters more than what you edit. The state of the part is kept at each point where the body is complete, so changing something near the top of the tree only rebuilds from there up — usually a fraction of a second, however big the part is. Changing something near the bottom rebuilds everything above it, because everything above it genuinely depends on it. That is why adding a new feature and adjusting it until it looks right stays quick even on a heavy model, while going back to move the very first sketch does not. Nothing needs doing to enable this; it is worth knowing only because it explains why two edits that look equally small can take very different amounts of time.

Views & projection

Normal to — Ctrl+8

Looks square down whatever plane is relevant: the sketch you have open, or a plane you have selected in the tree, or the Top plane if you haven't picked anything. This is the view you want for drawing and for reading dimensions — it's the only one where what you see on screen matches the true shape of the profile.

Isometric — Ctrl+7

The standard three-quarter view. Inside a sketch it tilts the camera off the sketch plane without closing the sketch, so you can see what you're drawing in context and then drop back with Ctrl+8.

Perspective and orthographic — Ctrl+5

The Persp / Ortho button swaps how the scene is projected.

  • Perspective converges, like an eye or a camera. Distant parts get smaller. It reads as more natural and gives a better sense of depth.
  • Orthographic keeps parallel edges parallel and holds scale across depth. Equal lengths measure equal wherever they sit, which is why engineering drawings use it, and why it's the projection to check a part in when you want to trust your eyes.

Both cameras share one orbit, so the swap holds your position and framing — only the projection changes.

Colours & the rendered view

Right-click the viewport and the Appearance section colours the model at three levels, each overriding the one above it:

  • Part colour paints every body that has no colour of its own.
  • Body colour — click the body, pick its colour. In a multi-body part (separate-body text, an import next to your model) each body can differ, which is also how you preview a multi-colour print.
  • Surface colour — click a single face. Faces have no permanent names in the kernel, so a painted face is re-found by its shape after every rebuild: edit something else and the colour stays put, but change the painted face itself — move it, resize it — and the colour lets go rather than guessing. Re-apply it with two clicks.

Colours save inside the .part file, follow the part into assemblies (an inserted part arrives in its part colour, and each component also has its own colour swatch in its Position panel), and carry through to the drawing's shaded isometric views — the sheet tints each body's faces with its colour.

The Render button in the toolbar (also in the right-click Appearance section) swaps the workshop look — matte grey, green edge lines — for a rendered one: your colours as polished metal in a studio, and no edge lines. It's presentation only; nothing about the model changes, and the choice is remembered. How bright that render is, and how much of the studio the metal picks up, are yours to set — see Lighting below.

Lighting

The small sun beside the Render button opens Lighting (it's also Lighting… in the right-click View section). The panel doesn't dim the viewport, because you're judging the model while you drag: move a slider and the picture follows the thumb.

  • Brightness — the exposure of the rendered view, 0.2 to 3. This is the one to reach for if a render looks dark. The curve is filmic, so turning it up lifts the mid-tones and rolls the highlights off rather than burning them to white; the gold on the demo lamp holds its colour across the whole range.
  • Environment — how much of the studio the metal reflects, 0 to 2. At 0 the rendered view has nothing to mirror and the part goes dark and plastic; turn it up for more sheen and stronger highlight streaks as you orbit.
  • Workshop light — the lamp the model is actually lit by, 0.1 to 1.2. This one works in both views: if the matte workshop view reads too dim on your screen, this is the slider that fixes it.

Four presets set all three together — Studio (the default), Bright, Soft and Moody. They're conveniences, not modes: click one to get near what you want, then move any slider from there and nothing snaps back. Reset returns the shipped defaults and forgets the setting.

Below the presets, Detail sets how finely curved faces are meshed, from ×1 to ×3. Curves in the viewport are drawn as many small flat steps, and at the default ×1 freyacad keeps them just fine enough to be quick — on a big smooth surface you can see the facets. Push Detail up and they melt away; the trade is that every rebuild takes longer, which is why this slider applies when you let go of it rather than following the thumb. The CAD geometry itself stays exact either way — dimensions and volumes don't move — and the same finer mesh feeds Print/Export, so a higher Detail also means smoother curved walls on the printed part. It can only refine: there's deliberately no coarser-than-default setting, so a print can never quietly come out rougher than the shipped look.

Lighting is remembered in this browser, on this computer — not in the part file, so a model you send someone opens under their lighting, not yours. Clearing the browser's site data forgets it, and so does Reset.

Making a sketch

One tool, one click: pick Sketch (under Features, in the Add menu or the right-click menu), and a prompt appears in the top right — click where the sketch goes: a flat face of the model, or one of the three origin planes, which show themselves for the pick. The camera flies round to look straight at it and the drawing tools appear. Esc, right-click or the prompt's Cancel backs out. On an empty part the planes are already showing, so you can simply click one — no tool needed.

While a sketch is open you'll see:

  • The origin — a small pair of arrows at 0,0. This is the anchor of your part; geometry tied back to it can't drift. The arrows flash when the sketch opens and whenever you resize, then fade so they don't clutter the view.
  • Plane traces — dashed, colour-keyed lines showing where the other two datum planes slice through the one you're on. They're the sketch equivalent of a centre line: extremely useful for keeping things symmetrical, and you can snap to them, drop points on them and dimension to them.
  • The status chip — a live count of what's still loose.

To round or bevel every corner of the profile at once, right-click and pick Sketch corners… (with the other sketch entity tools) — a fillet/chamfer control appears for just that job, and stays out of the way otherwise.

To treat one corner on its own, click it with the pointer. A ring marks the corner you picked and the panel gains a This corner section above the sketch-wide one:

ChoiceWhat it does
As sketchThe default — this corner follows whatever the whole sketch is set to. Picking it again removes the exception.
SquareLeaves this corner sharp even though the sketch is set to round or bevel. This is the one you can't express any other way.
Fillet / BevelThis corner gets its own radius or bevel, whatever the rest of the sketch does. A size box appears under it.

So a plate with three rounded corners and one square one is a sketch-wide fillet plus one corner set to Square, and a plate where every corner differs is four separate settings. The ends of an open run are not corners and are never offered. A corner setting travels with its point: move the point and it follows, delete the point or the shape and it goes too.

The right-click menu, and rearranging it

Right-clicking in a sketch gives four groups: Sketch (Finish, Finish + extrude, Cancel) first, since finishing is the commonest reason to open it; then Sketch entities, Relations and View.

Customise tools… at the foot of that menu opens a dialog where you can drag any tool into another group, reorder it, or drop it into Hidden to take it off both the menu and the dock. It has a tab per menu — Sketch, Model and Assembly — so every right-click menu in the program is arranged the same way, and each chip carries the tool's own icon so you can find it by sight. Reset this menu puts one back.

The last tab is Keyboard, where you can change any shortcut. Click the key beside a command and press the one you want; Esc cancels without changing anything. A key belongs to one command at a time, so if you give a key to something else, whatever had it gives it up — and if what it gives up was its own default, it is left with no shortcut rather than quietly taking the key back. reset beside a row restores that one command's default, and Reset all shortcuts restores the lot.

Editing, view and dialog keys are deliberately not rebindable: Esc, Enter, Delete, Backspace, the arrows, F1, F2 and Ctrl+Z/Y are conventions rather than preferences, and being able to reassign Esc is a good way to get stranded.

The same dialog has a Menu size control — Tiny, Compact, Normal or Large. It scales the floating dock and every right-click menu together, icons and text alike, so the sketch, model, assembly and drawing menus always match each other. Size applies the instant you click it and is kept even if you then press Cancel, because it is a display preference rather than part of the layout you are editing. The dock also wraps onto a second row rather than running off the side of the screen, however many tools you put in it.

Your layout is saved in the browser when you press Done, and survives a refresh, a new tab and a restart. What is stored is both the arrangement and the list of tools you deliberately hid — the second part matters, because it lets the program tell a tool you removed from a tool it gained in a later version. Ones you removed stay removed; genuinely new ones appear in the group they belong to, rather than staying invisible to the people who have customised the most.

Finish with Enter or the Finish button. Esc backs out of whatever you're mid-way through, and again to leave the sketch.

Closed profiles

Extrude and revolve need a closed loop. A chain of lines that doesn't return to its start has no inside, so there's nothing to give thickness to. If a feature refuses to build, an open profile is the first thing to check.

Worth knowing

The two axis-aligned rectangles are stored as rectangles, so they keep the width and height dimensions and the two corner handles. The two three-point ones can sit at any angle, which that storage can't express, so they commit as a closed four-sided profile — every corner is draggable, and you dimension the sides like any other lines.

Snapping

As the cursor nears something worth catching, it locks on and the target lights up before you click, so you always know what you're about to get. It snaps to:

  • the sketch origin;
  • plane traces, and the point where two of them cross;
  • endpoints of lines you've already drawn — these glow as you approach.

Dragging catches the same things. Pull an existing point around with the pointer and it hunts for the origin, the trace crossings, the traces and every other vertex, exactly as the drawing tools do — the marker turns teal when it has hold of something, and the status line names what it caught when you let go.

Snapping isn't cosmetic. A point snapped to the origin is genuinely attached to it, and that costs it its freedom to move — which is how a sketch becomes defined without you dimensioning every last thing. Dragging a loose endpoint onto the origin will define it the same way, mid-edit.

Snapping reaches a fixed distance on screen, not in millimetres, so zooming in tightens it: work up close and the cursor only catches what is genuinely near.

The cursor also shows a small glyph of the active tool up and to the right, so you can tell at a glance whether you're about to draw a line or a circle.

Dimensions

Press D or pick the dimension tool, then click what you want to measure. A greyed preview with real leaders and arrowheads follows the cursor, so you can see the dimension forming before you commit. Type a number and press Enter and the geometry moves to match — dimensions here drive the model, they don't just report it.

To dimensionClick
The length of a lineThe line, then Enter a value
Between two pointsOne endpoint, then another
A point back to the originThe point, then the origin
The angle between two linesOne line, then the other
The angle to a datum planeA line, then a plane trace (either order)
A point's distance to a datum planeThe point, then a plane trace (either order)
A circleThe circle — you get its diameter
A regular polygonOne of its sides — you get that side's length

Dimensioning a polygon by a side is what SolidWorks does, and it is the number you can actually measure on the finished part. Type a new side length and the polygon resizes about its centre, keeping its rotation: a hexagon of side 10 has corners 20 apart, and the program works that out for you. Older sketches that carry an across-corners diameter on a polygon still open and still read correctly.

The plane traces count as ground: they can't move, so a dimension to one costs only the sketched entity its freedom. That is what makes a line drawn from the origin fully defined by two dimensions — its length, and its angle to a plane. Two distances to the two traces do the same job for a single point, which is the reliable way to pin one down: a single diagonal distance leaves it free to swing on a circle.

Drag any dimension to reposition its leader line; long chains get unreadable fast if they all sit on top of each other. Values accept up to four decimal places, and the input box grows to fit what you type.

A dimension only changes when you type

This is a rule the whole program keeps, not a habit. Nothing — dragging a point, making a line horizontal, adding a relation — may alter a value you typed. If an operation cannot be carried out with every dimension still reading what it says, the operation is undone and you are told, rather than the numbers being quietly rewritten: "Horizontal would change a dimension — the sketch would be over-defined. Edit or delete the dimension first."

Driven dimensions

If a new dimension would over-define the sketch — what it measures is already fully decided — freyacad warns you and offers to add it as driven instead: a grey reference reading shown in parentheses, like (12.5 mm). A driven dimension follows the geometry and never moves it, costs no freedom in the count, and can be erased like any other. It's the honest way to keep a measurement visible on a sketch that's already fully in charge of itself.

Current limitation

Dimensions are applied one at a time, not solved together. Each one moves geometry to satisfy itself, which can disturb a dimension you set earlier — add an angle and a length you already fixed may shift. Work outward from the origin, dimension in the order you'd actually build the shape, and check earlier values afterwards. A proper constraint solver is the fix, and is the biggest single improvement still to come.

Constraints & degrees of freedom

The chip in the top-left corner is doing real arithmetic, not guessing. Every entity carries a number of free values — a line has four (two endpoints, two coordinates each), a circle three, a rectangle four — and every dimension, snap or constraint removes one. What is left over is the degrees of freedom: the number of independent ways your sketch can still deform.

When it hits zero the sketch is fully defined. That is the state you want before building on it, because nothing can shift underneath a feature later.

Only the origin and the datum planes count as ground. A shape can have every side dimensioned and every corner constrained to its neighbours and still slide around the plane as a rigid unit — internal relationships alone never fully define anything. Until something chains the shape to the origin or a plane (a coincident point, a distance to a plane, an angle to a plane), the count floors at the freedoms the whole shape retains, and the chip stays honest about it.

The constraints

Polygons join in fully: a polygon corner is a point like any other — make a line end coincident with one, dimension to it, or snap a line onto it while drawing — and the Horizontal / Vertical tools accept a polygon edge, spinning the whole polygon so that edge lies flat or stands upright (which is also how you put two corners vertically above each other). Anything touching or locked to a polygon's corners or centre rides along when the polygon is dragged, spun or stretched.

Five constraints live in the tool dock. Horizontal and vertical work either way round:

  • Select first — click a line with the pointer, then click the H or V icon.
  • Tool first — click the H or V icon, then click the line.

Horizontal and vertical are permanent: the line straightens and then stays that way — drag it and it slides or stretches, but it will not turn. A small green H or V badge appears up-right of the line's middle; the only way the line's angle changes again is deleting that badge (click it and press Delete, or click it with the eraser).

Coincident (⊕) locks two points together — click one point, then the point to lock it to, and from then on they move as one. Lock a point to the origin the same way to pin it to ground. Its badge sits by the joined point and deletes like the others.

Clicking a circle or polygon anywhere on its outline means its centre, so the same tool makes two of them concentric — no hunting for the little centre dot. That pairing badges itself ◎, since two shapes sharing a centre is worth being able to see and undo.

Snaps become constraints by themselves. Draw or drop a point onto the origin and a ⊕ appears; onto a plane trace and an ∈ (on-plane) appears — the point can then slide along the plane but never off it. Release a dragged point onto another point and the join is stored as a coincident. What snapped, holds — until you delete the badge.

Badges appear only for relationships you could not otherwise read off the sketch: ground (origin, plane) and concentric. Two line ends joined to each other show nothing — the join is plain to see, and a marker on every corner would only be clutter.

Parallel (∥) and equal (=) relate two lines, so they always want a reference first: click the icon (or have a line selected when you click it), click the line to copy from, then click the line to change. Parallel matches its direction; equal matches its length. Both persist exactly like H and V — the badge sits on the following line, and deleting it is what dissolves the relationship.

Every constraint holds through everything: drags, other constraints, and typed dimension changes all settle together, so a line that says H is horizontal — if a dimension edit would bend it, the free geometry moves instead.

Constraints respect what's already anchored. A line drawn from the origin is attached there, so making it vertical swings its free end round — it won't drag the anchored end off its datum. Only when neither end is held does the line pivot about its middle.

These are cheap and worth using freely. A line pinned horizontal loses a degree of freedom that no dimension then has to spend.

Dragging respects your dimensions

Only the origin and the datum planes are truly fixed. Everything else is exactly as free as its constraints leave it, and dragging honours that:

  • Undimensioned geometry drags anywhere.
  • Dimensioned geometry still drags — but the numbers hold. While you pull a point, every dimension keeps re-imposing its value. A line with a length dimension swings about its far end at exactly that length; two lines with an angle between them move and rotate as a pair, the angle intact. A drag can never silently rewrite a value you typed.
  • Fully defined geometry won't drag at all. Once every freedom is spent against the origin and planes, there is nowhere left to go — the point flashes red and the only way to move it is to edit a dimension.
  • And if a drag can't keep every number, it doesn't happen. When you let go, the sketch checks that every dimension still reads what you typed; a move that would have changed one is taken back, with a hint saying so. Typing is the only door a dimension's value can change through.

Points from different lines sitting on the same spot count as joined: drag a shared corner and everything meeting there moves together.

Why "fully defined" matters

An underdefined sketch isn't broken — you can extrude it perfectly well. The risk is later: change something upstream and an unconstrained profile can wander to a shape you never intended, and the first you'll know is a rebuild that looks wrong. Fully defined means the part can only change the way you asked it to.

Nothing in the sketch is coloured decoratively. Each colour means one thing:

Splines

The spline tool draws a smooth curve through the points you click — click each point in turn, and each one lands as you click it. Click the first point again to close the curve; Esc or Enter ends an open one, and Backspace steps back a point while you're drawing. A closed spline is a profile like any other: extrude it, cut with it, revolve it.

The curve is a centripetal fit, which is the one that doesn't overshoot or loop back on itself when your points are unevenly spaced. It passes exactly through every point, so what you clicked is where the curve goes.

Editing one

Spline points are ordinary sketch points. They drag, they snap, they take dimensions and constraints, they count against the degrees of freedom, and a dimension on one holds through a drag exactly like anywhere else. Select the spline with the pointer and it shows its workings: the control polygon it was fitted through, dashed, and a handle at every point.

  • Move a point — drag it.
  • Change the curve at a point — drag either end of that point's handle. The bar sets both the direction the curve leaves in and how hard it pulls; both ends stay in line, so the curve is always smooth through the point. A handle you have set yourself goes amber; the grey ones are the fit's own.
  • Insert a point — right-click on the curve where you want it.
  • Delete a point — right-click near it. A spline keeps a floor of two points (three if closed); below that, delete the whole spline.
  • Reset a handle, or all of them, from the same menu — the fit takes over again.
  • Open or close it, and hide the control polygons, from that menu too.
  • Delete with the spline selected removes the whole curve — unlike a chain of lines, a spline is one thing.

Splines mirror, take part in dynamic mirror, and can be construction geometry like anything else.

A spline reaches the geometry kernel as a true curve, not as a string of short straight pieces, and a whole spline arrives as one curve rather than one per span. So extruding or revolving one gives a single genuine curved surface: a STEP export carries real geometry rather than facets, a fillet has a proper surface to run along, and the model is far cheaper to build. See if a model feels slow for what does still cost time.

Where this stops

Not everything SolidWorks does with splines is here. These are through-point splines — SolidWorks' plain Spline tool. There are no control-point (style) splines, no equation-driven curves, no fitting a spline onto existing geometry, no curvature combs, and no tangent or curvature relations between a spline end and a neighbouring line: the handle is set by hand, not constrained to anything.

Editing a sketch

All of this needs the pointer tool active.

  • Move a point — drag any endpoint, as long as it's still blue. The view stays put while you drag, and the point snaps to the origin, the plane traces and other vertices on its way past.
  • Resize a circle or a polygon — drag its outline, anywhere along it. There's no special handle to find: the whole rim is the handle, and the shape follows the cursor from wherever you took hold. A single click on the outline selects the shape instead.
  • Spin a polygon — drag one of its corners. Corners resize and rotate; the flats between them only resize.
  • Delete — click a line to select it, then Delete. Or use the eraser to remove individual segments and dimensions.
  • Select several at once — drag a box over empty space. Everything completely inside it is caught, lines and dimensions alike; then press Delete or pick the eraser to remove the lot. (Hold Shift and drag if you want to orbit the view instead.)
  • Change a dimension — click its value and type a new one.
  • Turn a line into construction geometry — select it and tick Construction geometry. Ticking one segment of a longer chain splits that segment out first, so only the line you picked changes.

To edit a sketch you've already finished, find it in the tree and open it from the right-click menu. Everything downstream rebuilds when you're done.

A profile can be made of several pieces

A closed profile doesn't have to be one closed run. A line and a spline that meet at both ends enclose an area just as well, and so do four separate lines drawn as four separate runs — all of them extrude, cut and revolve. What matters is that the ends actually meet: snap each new run onto the end of the last, or join them with a coincident relation.

Where three or more ends meet at one point the shape is ambiguous, so nothing is guessed and no profile is made there. A run that dead-ends is simply left out — a stray line next to a closed rectangle doesn't stop the rectangle being a profile.

Features

Everything lives under Add. Features that need something they haven't got — a sketch, or an existing solid — stay greyed out until it exists.

Select a feature in the tree and its parameters appear in a panel — depth, radius, count, spacing. Edit them and the model rebuilds.

Worth knowing

Edges and faces select whole. The viewport draws and picks the model's real edges — the ones the geometry kernel knows about — not the display mesh's facets. Click anywhere on a circular edge and the entire circle highlights and fillets; click a face and the whole face answers, however coarsely the screen happens to tessellate it. Curved surfaces carry no stray lines at all.

Faces select like edges do. Move the cursor over the model and the face under it tints; click and it stays selected in blue until you pick something else. An edge within reach of the cursor wins, so the face answers for its interior. Then right-click for what that face can do: a flat one offers Sketch on this face, Text on this face, Normal to and Surface colour; a round one offers Surface colour and Add axis down its centre. A face and an edge are never selected at once — picking either lets the other go, and a click on empty space clears both.

You can only pick what you can see. An edge hidden behind the surface in front of it is never picked, however close the cursor passes to where it would be — so clicking the middle of a small face selects that face rather than something buried inside the solid. An edge on the silhouette still picks from either side of it.

Small flat faces forgive a near miss. The end of a swept tube can be a couple of millimetres across — a few pixels on screen, with the curved wall right beside it. When a click that needs a flat face lands just off one, freyacad takes the flat face whose centre is under the cursor rather than the wall the ray happened to graze. The hover highlight never does this: it always shows exactly what is under the pointer.

Round means round, not just cylindrical. Cones, spheres, tori and anything made by a revolve are read from the geometry kernel by name, so a revolved wall carries a real axis. Add axis and concentric mates take any of them, not only true cylinders.

Sketches with more than one shape

A sketch can hold several separate closed regions — say a hexagon with a hole and a triangle beside it. Extrude, cut and revolve normally use them all. When there is more than one, the feature's panel lists them as contours with a tick box each, so you can build from just the ones you want. Leave them all ticked for the usual behaviour.

A shape only counts as a contour if its outline is closed. An open chain has no inside, so it makes no face — that is the usual reason a shape seems to be ignored.

Worth knowing

Parameter-panel edits are covered by undo. Pressing Done records one step, so Ctrl+Z puts the old depth or radius back. Pressing Cancel, or Done without having changed anything, records nothing — so undo never fills up with entries for panels you only looked at.

Worth knowing

A distance × angle chamfer is built as its equivalent distance pair — the run along the second face is the first distance times the tangent of the angle. That is exact wherever the two faces meet square, which is every edge you would put an angle on. Open CASCADE has its own distance-angle routine, but it fails on the circular edges this app produces, and the distance pair does not.

Sweeping along a path

A sweep needs two sketches: the profile, which is the cross-section, and the path it travels. Draw the path first, then draw the profile on a plane the path starts on, and pick Swept boss or Swept cut. The panel's Path row lists every other sketch — it guesses the one before the profile, which is the order they are usually drawn in.

The profile is carried exactly as you drew it, staying square to the path the whole way. So where the profile sits matters: draw it centred on the start of the path and you get a tube down the middle of that path. If the path never reaches the profile's plane the feature says so rather than sweeping it off into space.

Round the corners in the path sketch. A path with a square corner sweeps to a mitred corner, which is right for a bar but not for a pipe. Give the path sketch a fillet and the bend comes out as a real bend — the path honours sketch fillets exactly as a profile does.

A profile with a hole in it sweeps as you would expect: two concentric circles give a tube with a bore, in one feature. A sweep can be mirrored and patterned like any other feature.

Worth knowing

The path has to be one connected run. Several lines drawn end to end are joined automatically, but a gap, a fork or a stray second line is refused rather than guessed at. A sweep also cannot twist along its path or change section as it goes, and it takes one path rather than a path plus guide curves. Where the section has to change, use a loft.

Lofting through profiles

A loft needs two or more sketches and skins a surface through them. Draw each section on its own plane — the origin planes, a datum plane, or a flat face of the model — then pick Lofted boss or Lofted cut. The panel's Profiles row is an ordered list: one drop-down per section, with ↑ and ↓ to move a section along the run, × to drop it and + Add a profile for the next.

The order is the shape. The skin runs through the sections top to bottom of that list, so moving a row is not tidying up — it builds a different solid. The numbers down the left are the run.

Ruled joins each pair of sections with straight sides, so three circles come out as two cones stacked. Leave it off — the default — and one smooth surface is fitted down the whole run, which is what turns the same three circles into a waisted neck.

One closed contour per sketch. A loft pairs its sections up wire by wire, and a sketch holding two loops gives no answer to which loop pairs with which. The biggest closed region of each sketch is the section; anything else in that sketch, holes included, is left out. Two loops that both want lofting are two lofts.

Worth knowing

There are no guide curves and no twist yet, and no start or end tangency condition — sections and their order are the whole control. Two sections on the same plane have nothing to skin between and the feature says so rather than building an empty solid. A loft can be mirrored and patterned like any other feature.

Text on a face

Pick Text on a face from Add and click any flat face — or right-click a flat face and choose it from the menu, which skips the pick. The panel opens with the text ready to type over, and the letters appear on the model as you type.

The first choice is whether the text is raised or sunk:

  • Raised text stands proud of the face. It can be part of the body — one solid, as if it were moulded in — or a separate body sitting on top.
  • Sunk text is engraved into the face. It can be just the cut, or the cut with a separate filler body that exactly fills it, flush with the face.

The separate-body options exist for multi-colour printing: export as 3MF and the lettering arrives in the slicer as its own object, so you can give it its own filament — raised letters in a second colour, or an engraving inlaid flush.

Bold, italic and underline work as anywhere else, and there are five faces to choose from — sans, serif, mono, round and narrow. They render from fonts already on your machine, so nothing downloads and nothing slows the app down.

Nothing is fixed at typing time. While the panel is open an amber handle sits on the text — drag it to place the text on its face by eye, and drag the smaller grip past the end of the letters to spin the text about its centre; the letter outlines follow the pointer and the solid catches up when you let go. The slide and angle fields are the exact-numbers version of the same moves, letter height and depth re-shape it whenever you come back, and Move to a different face… re-picks the face while keeping everything else. Starting the Text tool again while a text panel is open moves that text rather than starting a second one — press Done first if a second text is what you want. The letters become real solid geometry, so the text mirrors, patterns and exports like anything else you model.

What a mirror or pattern copies

Mirror, linear pattern and circular pattern all ask the same question, at the top of their panel:

CopyWhat you get
Whole bodyEverything built so far is reflected or repeated. Right for a symmetrical part you have modelled half of, or for laying out copies of a finished body.
Chosen featuresOnly the features you tick are copied. A tick list of every boss, cut, revolve and import above this point in the tree appears underneath.

The second is the one you want most of the time, and it is the difference between a bolt circle and a mess. Pattern one hole six ways round a disc and you get six holes. Pattern the whole body and you get six overlapping copies of the entire disc, hole and all — the wrong shape, and six times the work to build it.

Each copy is applied the way its own feature was applied: a boss adds material, a cut removes it. That is what makes a patterned hole cut more holes rather than deposit lumps. Tick a boss and a cut together and they go on in tree order, so the copied cut passes through the copied boss exactly as the original does.

Worth knowing

Fillets and chamfers can't be ticked. They reshape edges of the body rather than add a shape of their own, so there is nothing to copy — put the mirror or pattern before the fillet in the tree and the copies get rounded too, which is the usual order anyway. Merge result disappears when you choose features: a copied feature belongs to the body it came from, so there is nothing left to decide.

Parts saved before this existed open exactly as they did — no choice recorded means whole body. Delete a feature that something was copying and that tick simply drops off.

Fillets and chamfers are the usual place a rebuild fails, and it's rarely a bug: a radius bigger than the material it has to blend into has no valid solution. Reduce it, or put the fillet earlier in the tree before the geometry gets tight.

The feature tree

Build order, top to bottom. Click any item to select and highlight it.

Hover a row and the model answers. Point at any item in the tree — no click — and the thing it made lights up in the viewport: an origin plane or axis appears for as long as you hover it, a sketch glows in yellow even when it has been consumed and hidden — including an open path sketch, so you can see where a swept tube runs — a plane or axis you added glows, and a solid feature shows the shape it contributed as a translucent ghost. Where a feature has no separable shape of its own — a fillet rounds what is already there — the whole body tints instead, which still answers "that row is about this solid".

Naming things pays off quickly. Extrude 3 tells you nothing six features later; Mounting boss does. The demo part is named this way for exactly that reason.

Datum geometry

The three planes — Top, Front, Right — meet at the origin and are the reference everything else hangs off. They sit at the top of the tree, above your features, because they exist before the part does.

The three origin axes — X, Y and Z — sit just below them in the tree, and work the same way: click to show or hide, colour-keyed red, green and blue. Like the planes they are always there and never part of the recipe.

They're hidden until wanted; right-click one and toggle its visibility. You can also add your own:

  • Add plane — a new sketching plane set at an angle to one of the three. Pick the plane to start from, which of its two in-plane axes to tilt about, and the angle. The result still passes through that axis, so it's an angled plane rather than a floating one. This is how you sketch somewhere that isn't a face of the model.
  • Add axis — a reference line, useful as the spin axis for a revolve or a circular pattern. Its panel asks how the axis is defined: two planes (the line where they meet), normal to a plane through a point, a plain position and direction, or the centre of a circle — press the pick button and click anything circular on the model: a hole wall, a cylinder, a revolved wall, a round face, a rim, or just the arc of a rounded corner. The axis lands at its centre, pointing along it. Parallel planes are refused — they never meet.

Undo

Ctrl+Z steps back, Ctrl+Y forward, up to 60 steps. Undo captures the whole document, so stepping back into a sketch reopens that sketch as it was.

Parameter-panel edits count too: one step per Done that actually changed something. A new feature still takes a single press to remove, panel edit and all.

Saving & opening

Save keeps your documents in the browser — on your computer, inside the browser's own storage, with nothing installed. Open lists them: each document with its own icon (part, assembly, drawing), newest first. Click one to open it; the ⭳ on a row writes it out as a real file for sharing or backup, and Open from this computer… brings a real file in. A .part is plain JSON holding the entire recipe — every sketch, dimension, feature and parameter — and opens back up fully editable. An assembly saves as .assembly. Documents save under their names — open bracket.part, save, and it's bracket.part in the list. Files saved with the old .sketchcad / .asmcad names still open.

Worth knowing

The document list lives in this browser on this machine: it doesn't follow you to another computer, and clearing the browser's site data clears it. Anything you'd mind losing deserves a ⭳ download now and then — a real file is yours to back up and send.

Drawings reference their model, SolidWorks-style. Saving from a drawing writes a .drawing that holds the sheet — views, dimensions, marks — and the name of the part or assembly file it was drawn from, never the model itself. The model stays in its own file, saved separately, so keep the two files together. Opening a .drawing places the sheet straight over the referenced model if it's already open; otherwise freyacad asks you to pick the model file (or to use whatever model is open). A part picked this way is projected on the side, without disturbing the document you have open. Old .drawing files that embedded their model still open whole.

Open loads any of them back. New part starts fresh.

Worth knowing

To give these files freyacad's own icons in your file explorer — and open them by double-click — install freyacad as an app: in Edge or Chrome, the install button at the right end of the address bar (or menu → Apps → Install). Windows then associates .part, .assembly and .drawing with the app, each with its own icon. A web page can't change Explorer icons without that install — file type icons belong to installed apps.

Importing STEP and STL

The same Open button also takes .step / .stp and .stl files. They come into the current part as an import feature in the tree — the file's bytes travel inside your .part, so the part still saves, reopens and replays as one self-contained recipe.

  • STEP arrives as true B-rep solid geometry, read by the same kernel that writes it. An imported STEP body is a first-class citizen: fillet its edges, cut it, mirror it, pattern it, export it again.
  • STL is only ever triangles, so it arrives as a faceted body. It's exactly right for viewing, placing and re-exporting; fillets and smooth-surface work need real surfaces that a mesh simply doesn't have. STL files live in printer coordinates (Z up), and the import reads them that way — a benchy from the internet stands on its hull, and exporting then re-importing your own STL puts it back exactly where it was.

Files over 30 MB are refused — they'd make the saved part unwieldy. Decimate large scans first.

Worth knowing

The open document also keeps an autosave in the browser's local storage: every change is captured a moment after you make it, and the next time freyacad opens in this browser it picks up where you left off — a refresh, a crash or a closed tab doesn't lose the part. A demo you only looked at doesn't count as work, so it won't be "restored" at you. The net has limits: it's one slot, in this browser on this machine; clearing the browser's site data clears it too; and a part carrying a very large imported file is too big for it. Real work still deserves a real Save — a file is yours to keep, back up and send.

Units

The Units button switches what dimensions are displayed and typed in. It changes presentation, not the model — the underlying geometry is unaffected.

Printing & exporting

One button — Print / Export — opens a dialog with the three files that matter. Two promises hold for all of them: a dimension of 14 is 14 millimetres in the file, and the part arrives print-side down — whatever you built on the Top plane sits flat on the slicer's bed, the right way up.

3MF — for your slicer

The format Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer and PrusaSlicer prefer, and the one to reach for first. Unlike STL it says its units out loud, so a part can never import at the wrong size. The Open Bambu Studio button in the dialog launches the app if it's installed — then drop the downloaded file in, or just double-click the file.

STL — the universal mesh

A plain triangle mesh. Everything reads it. Curves become facets, so a cylinder is really a many-sided prism; fine for printing, lossy as a master copy.

STEP — for other CAD

Real B-rep geometry written straight out of the kernel: planes stay planes, cylinders stay cylinders, fillets stay analytic blend surfaces. Nothing is tessellated. This is the format to hand to a machine shop, to import into Fusion or SolidWorks, or to keep as a neutral archive.

None of the three carries feature history — keep the .part file too.

Worth knowing

Before 24 August 2026 the STL export wrote everything at ten times the modelled size while STEP was true size. If a print of an old export came out suspiciously large, that is why. Both are honest millimetres now.

Assemblies

New assembly starts a document that holds several parts rather than one. Insert brings a saved part in as a component; select one and Delete removes it. Assemblies save as .assembly.

Drag a component to slide it on the ground plane; hold Shift to raise or lower it instead. Right-click for the component menu — position, fix in place, remove, and the two mates. A component that is fixed won't move, and nothing mated to it can pull it: the first part you insert is fixed automatically, so everything else lands relative to it. Unfix a part and any mate that had nowhere to give settles straight away.

The origin

An assembly has the same origin a part does: Top, Front and Right planes and the X, Y and Z axes, at the head of the tree, click to show or hide. They are fixed — nothing moves them — which makes them the natural thing to build an assembly around. Parts can be mated to them, so you can sit a part on the Top plane or run it down the Y axis without needing another part to reference.

Mates

Both mates work by picking, one pick on each part. Choose the mate, click the face on the part you want to move, then click the face it should meet. Faces light up as the cursor passes over them, the first pick stays highlighted in amber, and Esc cancels. If a face is the wrong sort for the mate you chose, the pick is refused and says so rather than doing something surprising.

A concentric mate also takes circular edges, not just round faces: the rim of a hole, the lip of a boss, or even a rounded corner's arc. Hover one and a ring lights up around it; click and the mate lines up on that circle's centre and axis. It's the natural pick when the cylindrical wall is hidden or too thin to hit — a countersunk hole, a thin plate, a chamfered rim.

"Round" means anything with an axis: a cylinder, a cone, a torus, or a wall made by a revolve. What the mate needs is a shared axis, not a matching radius, so a tapered boss and a straight hole line up perfectly well.

The second pick can be the origin instead of another part: an origin plane for a coincident mate, an origin axis for a concentric one. Whichever sort the mate needs appears on its own as soon as you start it, so there's nothing to turn on first. The first pick is always a face on the part you want to move — the origin can't travel, so it can't be the thing that moves.

MatePickDoes
ConcentricTwo round faces or circular edges in any mix, or one of those and an origin axisPuts the two on a common axis — a pin into a hole, a boss into a counterbore, a shaft onto Y. The part stays free to slide along that axis and spin about it.
CoincidentTwo flat faces, or a flat face and an origin planeSets the faces against each other, face to face. The part stays free to slide about on that plane.

Faces are recovered from the part's own geometry, so a hole reads as a hole and its diameter is reported back when the mate lands. If both parts are fixed, nothing can move and the mate says so — and since the origin never moves, mating a fixed part to it is refused the same way. Otherwise the part whose face you picked first is the one that travels.

Mates hold

A mate is a relation, not a one-off move. Once it's on, it stays true: drag the part afterwards and it slides within whatever freedoms the mate leaves it, rather than coming loose. A pin mated concentric into a hole runs up and down that hole and spins in it, but will not come out sideways. Add a coincident mate on its end face as well and it's located outright, with only the spin left. Mates to the origin hold in exactly the same way — a part run down the Y axis stays on it however you drag.

Dragging a part tows its neighbours. The part under the cursor keeps its ground and anything mated to it that isn't fixed follows along, so a stack moves as a stack. Typing a position into the panel works the same way, and the boxes are corrected to where the part actually ended up — what the mates allow beats what you typed.

Every mate is listed under Mates in the tree, with an × to delete it; deleting one leaves the parts where they are. A mate that can't currently be met — usually because both its parts have been fixed — is marked with a ! and named in the status bar, along with how far out it is. If a new mate can't be met alongside the ones already there, it is refused and nothing moves, rather than half-applied.

Mates are saved with the assembly. Reopening the file replays the relations and solves the positions from them, so a mated assembly comes back mated.

Gridfinity

Gridfinity bin and Gridfinity baseplate sit in the Features menu and need no sketch: pick one and a real, editable solid lands in the tree. The panel speaks Gridfinity — width and depth in 42 mm units, height in 7 mm units, the stacking lip, compartments, a finger scoop — and the stack pitch stays exactly 7 mm × units, so bins made here nest with everyone else's. Magnet-free by design.

Because the bin is a genuine solid and not an imported mesh, everything else in the app works on it: sketch on the floor and cut a pocket, fillet an edge, pattern a cutout, then Print / Export. That is the difference between this and every mesh generator.

Tracing a real tool

Trace a photo… (in the sketch menu) turns a photo into an outline in the open sketch. The dialog fills the screen — tracing is precision work — with the tools grouped across the top in working order: the photo, helping the trace, fixing the line, real size, cut-outs. Lay the tool on a plain background, shoot square-on, pick the photo, and the traced outline appears. For scale, the honest way is Measure a line: click the two ends of something in the photo whose size you know — a ruler laid next to the tool is perfect — or drag from one end to the other, then type that line's real length. The readout under the photo shows the outline's true size, marked (measured). With no line drawn, the longest side box scales the outline by your estimate instead. Then insert, and cut it into a bin floor for a perfectly fitted pocket, or into anything else.

Fix-up tools when the automatic trace misses. Colours: press Pick object colour and click the tool in the photo (several shades is normal — a two-tone tool wants one click on each), then Pick background and click the background; the trace then separates by hue instead of brightness, which rescues a grey tool on a pale bench that no brightness threshold can split. The brush: grab the yellow line anywhere and drag — everything inside the brush circle follows, tapering off at the edges so the line bends rather than kinks. Straighten: click two points on the line and the wobbly stretch between them goes dead straight. Search area: drag a box on empty photo and the tracer looks only inside it — a plain click clears it. Undo steps back a stroke, Reset line returns to the trace as found, and the slider sets the brush size — touching it also hands the cursor straight back to the brush, so sizing and reshaping flow as one move.

Add finger hole puts a round notch on the outline — press it, set the radius, and click the line wherever you want to lift the part out of its pocket; click a hole again to take it away. On insert the notches are melted into the outline as one clean loop, sized in real millimetres.

Worth knowing

Tick dark background for a light tool on dark ground. The one thing the specialist version at freya.co.nz/grid still adds is the printable fit-check template; its bins open here — the Edit in freyacad button carries the configuration across.

Drawings

New drawing makes an engineering drawing of what you have modelled. It replaces the 3D view with a flat sheet of paper — nothing rotates, nothing orbits, the model is put away until you come back to it. The same button takes you back and forth; the drawing keeps its layout, and every view is re-projected from the model as it now stands each time you return. Change a dimension on the part, come back, and the drawing has changed with it.

Views

Add view offers front, top, right, left, bottom, back and isometric. Each is a real orthographic projection of the model's edges, not a picture of the screen: edges you can see draw solid, edges the solid stands in front of draw dashed as hidden detail, and Hidden turns that detail off. Drag a view to place it; the tree lists them with an × to remove one.

An isometric view is shaded by default, with its edges drawn on top — a wire cage of an isometric shows every edge of the far side straight through the near one, which is the one view where that helps nobody. The orthographic views stay line drawings, as a drawing should. Shaded / wireframe in the sheet menu switches the selected view either way.

Pull a view straight off another one. Hover a view and a dashed frame appears round it; drag from that frame and you get the view that belongs on the side you pulled towards — right for the right view, up for the top, a corner for isometric. It lands in line with its parent and stays there: drag a projected view and it slides along its one free axis, drag the parent and its projections come along. Which view you get follows the sheet's convention: third angle puts the top view above and the right view to the right, first angle the other way about, and the title block carries the matching symbol. Dragging from inside a view still just moves it, and Add view is still there when you want to name one.

Model… draws a saved part instead of the one you have open — pick a .part file and every view re-projects from it, without disturbing your work.

Dimensioning

Dimension (or D) arms the dimension tool. Click a corner on a view, click a second corner, then click where the line should sit. Corners snap, so a dimension always means the corner and not "roughly there". Where you place the line chooses the kind, as a smart dimension does: put it above or below and you asked for the horizontal distance, out to the side and you asked for the vertical one.

The corner the next click would take glows as the cursor passes it, so you are never aiming at a snap you cannot see. The number is measured off the model, not off the paper: change the sheet scale to 1:2 and the drawing halves while the dimension still reads 40. That holds on an isometric view too, where the picture itself is foreshortened: an isometric dimension is always aligned and reads the true corner-to-corner length of the part, not the shortened distance you could measure off the sheet with a ruler. Dimensions belong to their view, so they travel with it when you drag it and go with it when you delete it. Drag a dimension to move it where you want it; click one and press Delete to remove it. Esc puts the tool down, and again leaves the drawing.

Centre marks and centrelines

A hole on a drawing is expected to say where its centre is. Centre mark arms the tool; click the rim of any hole or round boss and it gets the standard cross, with chain-dashed arms reaching just past the rim. Centreline does the same job for round things seen from the side: click along a hole or a shaft and you get the chain-dashed line down its axis.

Mark every circle and axis does the whole of the selected view in one go, which is usually what you want. Run it again after changing the model and it only adds what is missing.

Both know what they are looking at because freyacad finds the model's circular edges in space, not on the paper. That is what lets one command serve both cases: a hole whose axis points at you is a circle on the sheet and takes a cross, and the same hole in the view next door is edge-on and takes a line down its middle — correctly along the hole, not across it. A hole at some angle in between draws as an ellipse and gets neither, which is what a draughtsman would do too.

Marks belong to their view exactly as dimensions do: they travel with it when you drag it, they stay the same size on the paper when you change the sheet scale, and they go when the view goes. Click one and press Delete to remove it. Esc puts the tool down.

Worth knowing

A round edge is recognised by being round, so a regular polygon of twelve or more sides would be taken for a circle. Six- and eight-sided bosses are safely below that line. And a mark records where the centre was when you placed it — move the hole in the model and the mark stays put, the same as a dimension does. Re-run Mark every circle and axis after a change of that kind.

The drawing tools

Right-click anywhere on the sheet for every drawing command, grouped the same way the sketch menu is, with the same floating dock along the top of the sheet. Customise tools… at the foot of that menu opens the same dialog the other modes use — the drawing has its own tab, and dragging a command into another group, or into Hidden, rearranges the menu and the dock together.

Sheet, scale and output

Sheet size, orientation and scale are in that menu and dock too — the top toolbar stays out of the way in this mode, since everything the sheet needs is on the sheet.

Sheet sizes run A4 to A0, portrait or landscape. The scale is one you pick from the usual series, or fit, which chooses the largest standard scale that still gets everything on the page — a drawing is read at a stated scale, so it picks 1:2 rather than inventing 1:1.83. Whatever it lands on is printed in the title block.

Print opens the sheet at its true paper size — print it, or use your browser's "Save as PDF". Export SVG saves it as vectors at real millimetre dimensions, which opens in Illustrator, Inkscape, or a laser cutter's software.

Where this stops

Dimensions are linear — between two corners. There are no diameter or radius dimensions and no angular ones, and no notes, balloons or bill of materials. There are no section, detail or auxiliary views. The drawing is not saved with the part: the sheet lasts as long as the tab, so print or export it before you close.

Known limits

Stated plainly, so nothing surprises you mid-part:

  • No constraint solver in sketches yet. Dimensions apply one at a time and can disturb each other. See the note under Dimensions. (Assembly mates are solved together — that solver doesn't work on sketch geometry.)
  • No perpendicular constraint. Horizontal, vertical, parallel, equal, concentric and coincident are in; perpendicular isn't yet.
  • STL imports are faceted. A mesh has no true surfaces, so an imported STL can be viewed, placed and exported but not filleted or chamfered.
  • Phones and tablets stay outside, by design. freyacad drives with a mouse, a keyboard and a hover cursor — use a PC or a Mac.
  • Drawings are linear dimensions only. No diameter, radius or angular dimensions, no notes or balloons, no section or detail views, and the sheet isn't saved with the part. Centre marks and centrelines are there — see Centre marks and centrelines.
  • Neither sweep nor loft takes a guide curve. A sweep carries one profile along one path at a constant section, and cannot twist as it goes. A loft does exist — an ordered run of two or more profiles, ruled or smooth — but it is driven by those sections and their order alone: no guide curves, no twist, no start or end tangency, and one closed contour per sketch. See Sweeping along a path and Lofting through profiles.
  • A pattern can't copy a fillet or chamfer. Bosses, cuts, revolves and imports can each be mirrored or patterned on their own; edge treatments can't, because they reshape the body rather than add a shape to it. Put the pattern before the fillet.
  • Only two assembly mates. Concentric and coincident hold as real relations and are solved together, but distance, angle, parallel and the rest aren't there yet, and neither are joints or sub-assemblies.

About

freyacad is built on Open CASCADE — the same B-rep kernel behind FreeCAD — compiled to WebAssembly and running in the page. That's why STEP export is genuine solid geometry rather than a mesh dressed up as one. The interface is drawn with three.js. The whole application is a single HTML file with no build step and no framework.
