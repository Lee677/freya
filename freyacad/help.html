freyacad

Parametric solid modelling that runs entirely in your browser.

Manual — last revised 22 August 2026

Start here

freyacad is a small CAD program with a real geometry kernel. You sketch on a plane, turn sketches into solids, and export STL for printing or STEP for other CAD. There is nothing to install and no account.

The workflow is the same one every mainstream CAD package uses, and it is worth knowing before you touch anything:

  1. Sketch a closed profile on a flat plane.
  2. Dimension it until it can't move.
  3. Extrude or revolve that profile into a solid.
  4. Repeat — each new feature builds on the last, and the whole stack rebuilds whenever you change something earlier in it.

That last point is what "parametric" means. Your part is not a mesh you sculpted; it is a recipe. Change a dimension near the bottom of the tree and everything above it is recalculated from scratch.

Good to know

Nothing leaves your machine. There is no server, no upload, and no telemetry. The geometry kernel runs as WebAssembly inside the page, so your part exists only in the tab until you save or export it — which also means closing the tab loses your work. Save early.

What you need

  • A desktop or laptop browser — Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari, reasonably current.
  • WebGL2 and WebAssembly, both of which those browsers have had for years.
  • A mouse with a wheel. A trackpad works, but a wheel makes zooming far nicer.

Phones and tablets are blocked deliberately. The kernel is an ~11 MB download and the interface needs a hover cursor and real screen space, so rather than let it half-work on a phone, freyacad shows a friendly note and stops.

The fastest way to learn it

Press Demo in the toolbar. It builds a finished part in front of you, feature by feature, so you can watch the tree fill in and see what a normal modelling session looks like. Then open the tree items and see how each was made.

Orbiting inside an open sketch works too, but only while the pointer tool is selected. That is deliberate: when a drawing tool is live, a drag should draw, not spin the model. Switch to the pointer and the view is yours again.

Views & projection

Normal to — Ctrl+8

Looks square down whatever plane is relevant: the sketch you have open, or a plane you have selected in the tree, or the Top plane if you haven't picked anything. This is the view you want for drawing and for reading dimensions — it's the only one where what you see on screen matches the true shape of the profile.

Isometric — Ctrl+7

The standard three-quarter view. Inside a sketch it tilts the camera off the sketch plane without closing the sketch, so you can see what you're drawing in context and then drop back with Ctrl+8.

Perspective and orthographic — Ctrl+5

The Persp / Ortho button swaps how the scene is projected.

  • Perspective converges, like an eye or a camera. Distant parts get smaller. It reads as more natural and gives a better sense of depth.
  • Orthographic keeps parallel edges parallel and holds scale across depth. Equal lengths measure equal wherever they sit, which is why engineering drawings use it, and why it's the projection to check a part in when you want to trust your eyes.

Both cameras share one orbit, so the swap holds your position and framing — only the projection changes.

Making a sketch

Add → Sketch, then pick a plane: Top, Front, Right, or a flat face of an existing solid. The camera flies round to look straight at it and the drawing tools appear.

While a sketch is open you'll see:

  • The origin — a small pair of arrows at 0,0. This is the anchor of your part; geometry tied back to it can't drift. The arrows flash when the sketch opens and whenever you resize, then fade so they don't clutter the view.
  • Plane traces — dashed, colour-keyed lines showing where the other two datum planes slice through the one you're on. They're the sketch equivalent of a centre line: extremely useful for keeping things symmetrical, and you can snap to them, drop points on them and dimension to them.
  • The status chip — a live count of what's still loose.

To round or bevel every corner of the profile at once, right-click and pick Sketch corners… — a fillet/chamfer control appears for just that job, and stays out of the way otherwise.

Finish with Enter or the Finish button. Esc backs out of whatever you're mid-way through, and again to leave the sketch.

Closed profiles

Extrude and revolve need a closed loop. A chain of lines that doesn't return to its start has no inside, so there's nothing to give thickness to. If a feature refuses to build, an open profile is the first thing to check.

Snapping

As the cursor nears something worth catching, it locks on and the target lights up before you click, so you always know what you're about to get. It snaps to:

  • the sketch origin;
  • plane traces, and the point where two of them cross;
  • endpoints of lines you've already drawn — these glow as you approach.

Dragging catches the same things. Pull an existing point around with the pointer and it hunts for the origin, the trace crossings, the traces and every other vertex, exactly as the drawing tools do — the marker turns teal when it has hold of something, and the status line names what it caught when you let go.

Snapping isn't cosmetic. A point snapped to the origin is genuinely attached to it, and that costs it its freedom to move — which is how a sketch becomes defined without you dimensioning every last thing. Dragging a loose endpoint onto the origin will define it the same way, mid-edit.

The cursor also shows a small glyph of the active tool up and to the right, so you can tell at a glance whether you're about to draw a line or a circle.

Dimensions

Press D or pick the dimension tool, then click what you want to measure. A greyed preview with real leaders and arrowheads follows the cursor, so you can see the dimension forming before you commit. Type a number and press Enter and the geometry moves to match — dimensions here drive the model, they don't just report it.

To dimensionClick
The length of a lineThe line, then Enter a value
Between two pointsOne endpoint, then another
A point back to the originThe point, then the origin
The angle between two linesOne line, then the other
The angle to a datum planeA line, then a plane trace (either order)
A point's distance to a datum planeThe point, then a plane trace (either order)
A circleThe circle — you get its diameter

The plane traces count as ground: they can't move, so a dimension to one costs only the sketched entity its freedom. That is what makes a line drawn from the origin fully defined by two dimensions — its length, and its angle to a plane. Two distances to the two traces do the same job for a single point, which is the reliable way to pin one down: a single diagonal distance leaves it free to swing on a circle.

Drag any dimension to reposition its leader line; long chains get unreadable fast if they all sit on top of each other. Values accept up to four decimal places, and the input box grows to fit what you type.

Driven dimensions

If a new dimension would over-define the sketch — what it measures is already fully decided — freyacad warns you and offers to add it as driven instead: a grey reference reading shown in parentheses, like (12.5 mm). A driven dimension follows the geometry and never moves it, costs no freedom in the count, and can be erased like any other. It's the honest way to keep a measurement visible on a sketch that's already fully in charge of itself.

Current limitation

Dimensions are applied one at a time, not solved together. Each one moves geometry to satisfy itself, which can disturb a dimension you set earlier — add an angle and a length you already fixed may shift. Work outward from the origin, dimension in the order you'd actually build the shape, and check earlier values afterwards. A proper constraint solver is the fix, and is the biggest single improvement still to come.

Constraints & degrees of freedom

The chip in the top-left corner is doing real arithmetic, not guessing. Every entity carries a number of free values — a line has four (two endpoints, two coordinates each), a circle three, a rectangle four — and every dimension, snap or constraint removes one. What is left over is the degrees of freedom: the number of independent ways your sketch can still deform.

When it hits zero the sketch is fully defined. That is the state you want before building on it, because nothing can shift underneath a feature later.

Only the origin and the datum planes count as ground. A shape can have every side dimensioned and every corner constrained to its neighbours and still slide around the plane as a rigid unit — internal relationships alone never fully define anything. Until something chains the shape to the origin or a plane (a coincident point, a distance to a plane, an angle to a plane), the count floors at the freedoms the whole shape retains, and the chip stays honest about it.

The constraints

Six constraints live in the tool dock. Horizontal and vertical work either way round:

  • Select first — click a line with the pointer, then click the H or V icon.
  • Tool first — click the H or V icon, then click the line.

Horizontal and vertical are permanent: the line straightens and then stays that way — drag it and it slides or stretches, but it will not turn. A small green H or V badge appears up-right of the line's middle; the only way the line's angle changes again is deleting that badge (click it and press Delete, or click it with the eraser).

Coincident (⊕) locks two points together — click one point, then the point to lock it to, and from then on they move as one. Lock a point to the origin the same way to pin it to ground. Its badge sits by the joined point and deletes like the others.

Snaps become constraints by themselves. Draw or drop a point onto the origin and a ⊕ appears; onto a plane trace and an ∈ (on-plane) appears — the point can then slide along the plane but never off it. Release a dragged point onto another point and the join is stored as a coincident. What snapped, holds — until you delete the badge.

Parallel (∥) and equal (=) relate two lines, so they always want a reference first: click the icon (or have a line selected when you click it), click the line to copy from, then click the line to change. Parallel matches its direction; equal matches its length. Concentric (◎) does the same for circles and polygons: click the reference, then the one to move onto its centre. All of these persist exactly like H and V — the badge sits on the following line (or the shared centre), and deleting it is what dissolves the relationship.

Every constraint holds through everything: drags, other constraints, and typed dimension changes all settle together, so a line that says H is horizontal — if a dimension edit would bend it, the free geometry moves instead.

Constraints respect what's already anchored. A line drawn from the origin is attached there, so making it vertical swings its free end round — it won't drag the anchored end off its datum. Only when neither end is held does the line pivot about its middle.

These are cheap and worth using freely. A line pinned horizontal loses a degree of freedom that no dimension then has to spend.

Dragging respects your dimensions

Only the origin and the datum planes are truly fixed. Everything else is exactly as free as its constraints leave it, and dragging honours that:

  • Undimensioned geometry drags anywhere.
  • Dimensioned geometry still drags — but the numbers hold. While you pull a point, every dimension keeps re-imposing its value. A line with a length dimension swings about its far end at exactly that length; two lines with an angle between them move and rotate as a pair, the angle intact. A drag can never silently rewrite a value you typed.
  • Fully defined geometry won't drag at all. Once every freedom is spent against the origin and planes, there is nowhere left to go — the point flashes red and the only way to move it is to edit a dimension.
  • And if a drag can't keep every number, it doesn't happen. When you let go, the sketch checks that every dimension still reads what you typed; a move that would have changed one is taken back, with a hint saying so. Typing is the only door a dimension's value can change through.

Points from different lines sitting on the same spot count as joined: drag a shared corner and everything meeting there moves together.

Why "fully defined" matters

An underdefined sketch isn't broken — you can extrude it perfectly well. The risk is later: change something upstream and an unconstrained profile can wander to a shape you never intended, and the first you'll know is a rebuild that looks wrong. Fully defined means the part can only change the way you asked it to.

Reading the colours

Nothing in the sketch is coloured decoratively. Each colour means one thing:

Points follow the same rule as the lines they belong to, so a blue dot can be dragged and an amber one can't. Dimensions keep a hue of their own precisely so they can never be mistaken for geometry in any state.

Editing a sketch

All of this needs the pointer tool active.

  • Move a point — drag any endpoint, as long as it's still blue. The view stays put while you drag, and the point snaps to the origin, the plane traces and other vertices on its way past.
  • Resize or spin a polygon — drag one of its corners.
  • Delete — click a line to select it, then Delete. Or use the eraser to remove individual segments and dimensions.
  • Change a dimension — click its value and type a new one.

To edit a sketch you've already finished, find it in the tree and open it from the right-click menu. Everything downstream rebuilds when you're done.

Features

Everything lives under Add. Features that need something they haven't got — a sketch, or an existing solid — stay greyed out until it exists.

Select a feature in the tree and its parameters appear in a panel — depth, radius, count, spacing. Edit them and the model rebuilds.

Worth knowing

Edits made in the parameter panel are not covered by undo. Ctrl+Z tracks sketching and tree operations; it won't walk back an extrude depth you retyped. Note the old number before you change it.

Fillets and chamfers are the usual place a rebuild fails, and it's rarely a bug: a radius bigger than the material it has to blend into has no valid solution. Reduce it, or put the fillet earlier in the tree before the geometry gets tight.

Naming things pays off quickly. Extrude 3 tells you nothing six features later; Mounting boss does. The demo part is named this way for exactly that reason.

Sketches nest inside the feature built from them, since once consumed they're an ingredient rather than a step of their own. They're hidden by default; right-click → Show displays one in yellow when you need to see what a feature was made from. An eye icon on the row marks anything currently visible.

Datum geometry

The three planes — Top, Front, Right — meet at the origin and are the reference everything else hangs off. They sit at the top of the tree, above your features, because they exist before the part does.

They're hidden until wanted; right-click one and toggle its visibility. You can also add your own:

  • Add plane — a new sketching plane, typically offset from an existing one. This is how you sketch somewhere that isn't a face of the model.
  • Add axis — a reference line, useful as the spin axis for a revolve or a circular pattern.

Undo

Ctrl+Z steps back, Ctrl+Y forward, up to 60 steps. Undo captures the whole document, so stepping back into a sketch reopens that sketch as it was.

The exception is the parameter panel, as noted above.

Saving & opening

Save writes a .sketchcad file — plain JSON holding the entire recipe: every sketch, dimension, feature and parameter. It's small, diffable, and opens back up fully editable. This is your working format; use it for anything you might revisit.

Open loads one back. New part starts fresh.

Importing STEP and STL

The same Open button also takes .step / .stp and .stl files. They come into the current part as an import feature in the tree — the file's bytes travel inside your .sketchcad, so the part still saves, reopens and replays as one self-contained recipe.

  • STEP arrives as true B-rep solid geometry, read by the same kernel that writes it. An imported STEP body is a first-class citizen: fillet its edges, cut it, mirror it, pattern it, export it again.
  • STL is only ever triangles, so it arrives as a faceted body. It's exactly right for viewing, placing and re-exporting; fillets and smooth-surface work need real surfaces that a mesh simply doesn't have.

Files over 30 MB are refused — they'd make the saved part unwieldy. Decimate large scans first.

Save early

There is no autosave and no recovery. Everything lives in the tab, so a crash, a closed window or a stray refresh takes the part with it. Save whenever you've made progress you'd be annoyed to redo.

Units

The Units button switches what dimensions are displayed and typed in. It changes presentation, not the model — the underlying geometry is unaffected.

Exporting

STL — for 3D printing

A triangle mesh. Every slicer reads it, and it's what you want for printing. Curves become facets, so a cylinder is really a many-sided prism; fine for printing, lossy as a master copy. STL carries no feature history — keep the .sketchcad file too.

STEP — for other CAD

Real B-rep geometry written straight out of the kernel: planes stay planes, cylinders stay cylinders, fillets stay analytic blend surfaces. Nothing is tessellated. This is the format to hand to a machine shop, to import into Fusion or SolidWorks, or to keep as a neutral archive. If you're choosing between the two and not printing, choose STEP.

Assemblies

New assembly starts a document that holds several parts rather than one. Insert brings a saved part in as a component; select one and Delete removes it. Assemblies save as .asmcad.

This is the newest and least developed corner of freyacad — treat it as useful for laying parts out together and checking how they sit, rather than as a full assembly environment with mates and joints.

Known limits

Stated plainly, so nothing surprises you mid-part:

  • No constraint solver yet. Dimensions apply one at a time and can disturb each other. See the note under Dimensions.
  • No perpendicular constraint. Horizontal, vertical, parallel, equal, concentric and coincident are in; perpendicular isn't yet.
  • STL imports are faceted. A mesh has no true surfaces, so an imported STL can be viewed, placed and exported but not filleted or chamfered.
  • The parameter panel is outside undo.
  • Desktop only, by design.
  • No autosave. The tab is the only copy until you save.
  • Assemblies are early. No mates or joints.

About

freyacad is built on Open CASCADE — the same B-rep kernel behind FreeCAD — compiled to WebAssembly and running in the page. That's why STEP export is genuine solid geometry rather than a mesh dressed up as one. The interface is drawn with three.js. The whole application is a single HTML file with no build step and no framework.
